# Short field takeoffs and landings

---

## Aim

*   To verify, by calculation, that there is adequate runway for takeoff and landing in accordance with the plane's POH
*   To learn to fly a short field takeoff and landing

---

## Motivation

*   Short field technique is necessary:
    *   When the runway is relatively short
    *   When there are obstacles at the approach or departure ends of the runway

<!--
Why might we want to use short field takeoff or landing technique?

When we talk about a runway being short, we mean short relative to the performance of the aircraft on the day. A runway may be relatively long for one aircraft while still being relatively short for another. A runway may also usually be fine for your aircraft, but may be too short on a particularly hot day with low atmospheric pressure and enough passengers and luggage to bring the plane to MTOW.

What do these runway markings mean? Displaced threshold—obstacles infringe the approach path and aircraft may not land in the area behind the displaced threshold. In the case of Warnervale, we have trees which infringe on the approach path, and that is why our threshold is displaced. This obviously reduces the available runway for landing.
-->

## Factors affecting takeoff and landing performance

---

## Definitions

*   **Takeoff distance**
    *   The total horizontal distanced traveled from the start of the takeoff to the point where the aircraft is 50ft above the ground
*   **Landing distance**
    *   The total horizontal distance traveled from the point where the aircraft is 50ft above the ground until it comes to a complete stop on the runway surface

---

## Air density

*   Air density is a measure of the mass of air in a given volume
*   As air density decreases, both engine and aerodynamic performance decrease
*   Atmospheric pressure decreases as altitude increases
*   As temperature increases, air expands and becomes less dense
*   Air density also decreases as humidity increases
*   Weather systems affect atmospheric pressure over a large area
*   Hot, high, humid, and a low pressure system = worst case

## Runway surface

*   Different types of surface:
    *   Paved
    *   Grass (short vs long)
    *   Gravel, sand, etc.
    *   Dry vs wet
    <!-- Why might surface affect our takeoff and landing distance? -->
*   Affects braking traction
    <!-- Wet surfaces, particularly wet grass, have significantly less braking traction and will increase braking distance on landing -->
*   Affects rolling resistance
    <!-- Soft surfaces like long grass and soft sand increase rolling resistance, increasing takeoff distance and reducing landing distance -->

---

## Slope

*   Slope affects acceleration and braking distance
*   Better to takeoff downhill and land uphill

<!-- How would you think an upsloping runway would affect acceleration? What about braking? It's the opposite for a downsloping runway. Would we get a shorter takeoff distance by going uphill or downhill? What about landing distance? -->

## Wind

*   Specifically, not the entire wind, but the component acting down the runway
    *   We will learn how to determine this shortly
*   A headwind component reduces takeoff roll and landing distance

![bg right contain](./windsock.jpg)

---

## Weight

*   Increased weight slows acceleration during the takeoff roll, and reduces climb performance
*   Although weight increases our momentum, it also increases braking traction. Overall, weight tends to increase landing distance, but the effect is less significant than on takeoff.

---

## Flaps

*   Flaps reduces the stalling speed and can enable takeoff at a lower airspeed
*   This can reduce ground-roll, but may not reduce takeoff distance to 50ft due to the reduced rate-of-climb
*   Always use the configuration specified in your plane's POH
    *   In the Cessna 150 flaps zero is specified for a short field takeoff

## International Standard Atmopshere (ISA)

*   A static model of how pressure, temperature, etc. vary over a range of altitudes
*   Provides a standard reference for temperature and pressure
*   Useful for comparison—current conditions vs a "standard" day
*   Standard sea level temperature = 15°C
*   Standard sea level atmospheric pressure = 1013.2 hPa

---

## Pressure height

*   All aircraft performance data in a POH is in relation to the ISA
*   However, actual conditions at a particular aerodrome rarely match the ISA as every day we have variations in atmospheric pressure
*   Therefore we calculate **pressure height**:
    *   The height in the standard atmosphere where pressure is the same as it currently is at a particular aerodrome

<!-- Draw field on cliff, show "standard" day with QNH 1013 at sea level and field elevation, then "low pressure" day with QNH 1000—1013 is lower, and so the height of the field over 1013 is greater. That is our pressure height. If we just concerned ourself with barometric pressure, it is as if the field is at a higher elevation. -->

---

## Calculating pressure height

$$
Pressure\ height = Elevation + (1013 - QNH) \times 30
$$

<!--
To calculate pressure height we use this formula.

Elevation refers to the height of the aerodrome above sea level.

QNH refers to the atmopsheric pressure at sea level.

30 is a constant, and refers to the pressure lapse rate i.e. how much pressure drops as altitude increases. We assume that pressure drops 1hPa for every 30ft of height increase above sea level.
-->

---

## Calculating pressure height

For example, Warnervale with QNH 980 hPa:

$$
\begin{align}
Pressure\ height &= Elevation + (1013 - QNH) \times 30
\end{align}
$$

## Calculating pressure height

For example, Warnervale with QNH 980 hPa:

$$
\begin{align}
Pressure\ height &= Elevation + (1013 - QNH) \times 30 \\
Elevation &= 25 \\
QNH &= 980 \\
\\
Pressure \ height &= 25 + (1013 - 980) \times 30
\end{align}
$$

<!-- Punch that into your calculator, what do you get? 1015 is the pressure height! -->

---

## Calculating pressure height

For example, Warnervale with QNH 980 hPa:

$$
\begin{align}
Pressure\ height &= Elevation + (1013 - QNH) \times 30 \\
Elevation &= 25 \\
QNH &= 980 \\
\\
Pressure \ height &= 25 + (1013 - 980) \times 30 \\
&= 1015
\end{align}
$$

<!-- Therefore, in this example the atmospheric pressure at Warnervale is the same as the atmospheric pressure in the standard atmosphere at an altitude of 1015ft. So would our performance be better or worse than we'd expect on a "standard" day? -->
